# Glutt Skills — Culinary Instructor Deliverable

**Role used for this document:** cooking instructor responsible for deciding what a beginner should be taught, what should be corrected, what should be left alone, and what can honestly be judged through Glutt's first-person smart-glasses camera.

**Operating rule:** permissive by default. A difference is not an error until it has a meaningful cost in safety, control, even cooking, texture, flavor, or repeatability.

**Evidence labels used below**
- **[SRC]** = anchored in professional culinary instruction, food-safety guidance, or cooking-science sources listed in the Source Notes.
- **[INST]** = my instructor judgement: the standard I would actually hold a home cook to.
- **[DISAGREE]** = competent instructors/schools legitimately teach more than one approach; Chef must not present one as settled fact.

---

# 0. Changes I would make to the skill map before writing rubrics

1. **Keep "Hold a Chef's Knife" and "Claw Grip" separate.** Good.
2. **"Slice" should teach the cutting motion, not a particular food shape.** A push cut, pull/slice, and rock are all legitimate depending on knife, ingredient, and school. The outcome is a clean cut with control, not compulsory rocking. [SRC][DISAGREE]
3. **"Dice" should be outcome-first.** Professional dimensions remain reference targets, but home mastery should mean reasonably cubic and consistent enough to cook evenly. Rouxbe explicitly distinguishes culinary-school precision from what matters at home. [SRC]
4. **"Dice an Onion" must accept both the traditional horizontal-cut method and the safer no-horizontal-cut method.** [SRC][DISAGREE]
5. **"Mirepoix" is not primarily a knife-skill challenge.** It is a *functional cut + ratio + sweating* challenge. If this challenge is meant to remain in Knife Skills, grade the cut only; otherwise move it later to flavor foundations.
6. **"Stove Levels" should be renamed `Control Heat on Your Stove`.** Dial numbers are not portable across burners, pans, induction, gas, and electric. Teach response cues.
7. **"Pan Ready" and "Preheat" are distinct but adjacent:** preheat = bringing pan toward working state; pan ready = proving the state is appropriate for the food/fat about to enter.
8. **"Oil" should be renamed `Read Oil in a Pan`.** The core skill is amount + movement + heat response, not a generic lesson about oil.
9. **"Butter" should be renamed `Read Butter in a Pan` or split later into `Cook With Butter` and `Brown Butter`.**
10. **"Doneness" is too broad to be one visually mastered skill.** Keep it as a judgement lesson, but Chef should never certify protein doneness from color alone. For proteins, thermometer skill outranks visual doneness. [SRC]
11. **"Taste as You Go," "Balance a Sauce," and most Flavor/Intuition skills cannot be visually certified.** They can be *coached* by camera + dialogue but not "watched correct" in the same sense as claw grip.
12. **Do not count one correct performance as learned.** See Mastery section at the end.

# 1. WRITTEN SKILLS — VISUAL/AUDIO RUBRICS

## KNIFE SKILLS

### 1. Claw Grip

**Best assessment:** process, with several frames while the guiding hand advances.

**targetTechnique [SRC][INST]**
- Guiding hand rests on top/side of the ingredient rather than in front of the cutting path.
- Fingertips are curled or otherwise retracted behind the forward knuckles.
- Thumb is behind the fingers, not protruding toward the blade.
- A knuckle/flat side of the guiding fingers can act as a reference for the blade.
- The food is stable enough that the hand is not chasing or pinning a rolling ingredient unsafely.

**acceptableVariations**
- Fingertips do not have to form an exaggerated "perfect claw"; large hands, small hands, arthritis, long nails, and ingredient geometry change the shape.
- Index, middle, or a combination of knuckles may be the forward guide.
- For large stable items, the hand may lie flatter *while the blade is far away*; do not call that wrong until the cut enters the hand's vicinity.
- Thumb position varies, provided it remains behind the cutting line.
- Very small ingredients may be held with a compact pinch rather than a textbook claw if fingers remain safely clear.
- Left-handed use is mirrored.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `fingertipsAhead` — **Obs:** one or more fingertips project past the knuckles into the blade's path. **Say:** “Curl those fingertips back behind your knuckles before the next cut.” **Why:** this is the direct laceration risk. **Context:** general. **Visibility:** fingertip/edge relationship must be visible.
2. `thumbForward` — **Obs:** thumb sticks forward alongside the ingredient near the cut line. **Say:** “Pull your thumb back behind your fingers; it’s the one I don’t want near the blade.” **Why:** beginners protect the fingers and forget the thumb. **Context:** general. **Visibility:** thumb and blade path.
3. `handInFrontOfBlade` — **Obs:** guiding hand is pinching/holding food on the side the knife is moving toward. **Say:** “Move your holding hand behind the knife, not in front of where it’s traveling.” **Why:** path safety.
4. `rollingFood` — **Obs:** round ingredient rolls while the knife descends. **Say:** “Give that a flat side first so it can’t roll under the knife.” **Why:** stability beats hand gymnastics. **Context:** ingredient-specific.

**safetySignals**
- Visible fingertip/thumb under or directly against the cutting edge.
- Food rolls and blade visibly slips toward the guiding hand.
- Cook attempts to catch a falling knife. Chef: “Let it fall—hands away.”
- Cutting toward the palm while holding food in the air for a task that can be board-cut.

**notVisuallyAssessable / ask**
- Excessive squeeze/tension: “Does your guiding hand feel relaxed enough to slide back as you cut?”
- Sharpness: “Does the knife bite cleanly, or are you having to force it?”

**parts:** fingertips back / thumb back / knuckle-forward reference / food stable.

**framing:** “Look down at your guiding hand and make three slow cuts. Keep the whole hand and the knife edge in view.”

### 2. Slice

**Best assessment:** process + finished cut surface.

**targetTechnique [SRC][INST]**
- Blade travels through the food with a smooth slicing component rather than repeatedly crushing straight down.
- Cutting path is deliberate and controlled.
- Guiding hand remains clear.
- Result has relatively clean faces rather than torn/crushed edges.

**acceptableVariations [DISAGREE]**
- Push-cut, pull/slice, gentle rock, or Japanese-style up/down push cut may all be correct.
- Tip may stay on board in a classic rock or lift fully with a flatter-profile knife.
- Stroke length changes with knife length and ingredient.
- Soft foods may need almost no downward force; dense foods need more.
- A bread/tomato task belongs to a serrated knife and sawing action, not this chef-knife rubric.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `unsafeGuideHand` — same safety priority as claw grip.
2. `crushNotSlice` — **Obs:** blade repeatedly stamps down; food visibly compresses/tears. **Say:** “Let the blade travel forward or back as it goes down—slice through it instead of pressing straight through.”
3. `wildLift` — **Obs:** blade lifts unnecessarily high and lands inconsistently. **Say:** “Keep the knife closer to the board and shorten the stroke until it feels controlled.”
4. `sawingShort` — **Obs:** many tiny frantic back-and-forth strokes on ordinary produce. **Say:** “Use one longer, smoother stroke instead of sawing at it.”

**outcome tolerance**
- Professional: clean, consistent target thickness.
- Home mastery: most slices within roughly ±20% of intended thickness; obvious outliers matter more than ruler precision. [INST]

**audio opportunity:** repeated hard board impacts indicate stamping; a clean carrot cut often has a single crisp fracture rather than multiple forced knocks, but audio should support—not independently fail—the student.

**framing:** “Give me five normal slices at half speed, then show me the cut faces.”

### 3. Rough Chop

**Best assessment:** outcome.

**targetTechnique [INST]**
- Pieces are intentionally rustic but within a useful size band for the dish.
- No giant intact chunks hiding among tiny fragments.
- Safe, controlled knife work.

**acceptableVariations**
- Shape is irrelevant.
- Exact dimensions are irrelevant.
- Different foods naturally fracture differently.
- Rock chop, push cut, or cross-chop can all be fine.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `dangerousChop` — unsafe hand/blade relationship. “Slow down and put your holding hand behind the blade path.”
2. `sizeChaos` — **Obs:** mixture contains pieces several times larger than the majority. **Say:** “Find the biggest pieces and bring just those down to the size of the rest.”
3. `overChopped` — **Obs:** intended rough chop has become mince/paste. **Say:** “Stop there—you’ve already got it small enough for a rough chop.”

**outcome tolerance [INST]**
- Home: aim for the largest normal piece no more than about 2× the width of the smallest normal piece; ignore a few flakes/crumbs.
- Do not use a professional ruler standard for “rough.”

**framing:** “Finish the chop, spread it once with the side of the knife or a scraper, and look straight down at the board.”

### 4. Dice

**Best assessment:** outcome first, process second.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Pieces are cube-like with reasonably square faces.
- Pieces are consistent enough to cook at similar rates.
- Classical references: small 1/4 in (6 mm), medium 1/2 in (12 mm), large 3/4 in (19 mm). [SRC]
- Knife is reasonably perpendicular where square cuts are intended.

**acceptableVariations**
- For ordinary home recipes that simply say “dice,” exact classical size is not required. Rouxbe explicitly treats equal-size cooking pieces as sufficient for home cooks. [SRC]
- Curved produce will create edge pieces that are not cubes unless aggressively trimmed; do not penalize useful food to reward waste.
- A recipe-specific “dice” can be any stated size.
- Softer foods (tomato, avocado) will have less geometric edges.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `unsafeHand`.
2. `unevenCookingSize` — **Obs:** repeated large and small pieces mixed. **Say:** “Use one finished piece as your size guide and match the next few cuts to it.”
3. `strongWedges` — **Obs:** pieces consistently taper because knife is leaning. **Say:** “Bring the blade upright—your cuts are leaning and turning cubes into wedges.”
4. `crushing` — **Obs:** food crushed/smeared rather than cut. **Say:** “Use a sharper slicing stroke and less downward force.”

**outcome tolerance [INST]**
- Professional exercise: target classical dimensions ±10–15% and visibly square.
- Glutt home mastery: central ~80% of pieces within ±25% of the chosen size; reject only obvious inconsistency that changes cooking.
- For non-squared natural vegetables, judge *cooking dimension* (thickness) more than perfect cube geometry.

**framing:** “Spread one handful into a single layer and look straight down. I’m checking size consistency, not perfection.”

### 5. Mince

**Best assessment:** outcome.

**targetTechnique**
- Food is cut into very small pieces appropriate to its use without needless pounding.
- Distribution is fairly even; for herbs, avoid bruised wet paste unless a paste is intended.
- For aromatics, “fine enough to disperse” matters more than an arbitrary millimeter.

**acceptableVariations**
- Rock-chop, repeated slice-and-gather, cross-chop.
- Garlic may be minced with knife, crushed then minced, grated/pressed when the *recipe* wants stronger extraction; these produce different flavor, so do not call them identical. [SRC]
- Some minces intentionally become paste with salt/knife.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `unsafeGather` — hand near edge while gathering. “Set the knife down or use the flat/bench scraper to gather before you continue.”
2. `largeStragglers` — “Pull the big pieces into the center and give only those a few more passes.”
3. `poundingHerbs` contextual — “Switch from pounding straight down to clean slices; you’re bruising the herbs into the board.”

**outcome tolerance [INST]**
- Home: no repeated pieces larger than about 3–4 mm when a “fine mince” is intended; exact brunoise is a different skill.
- Judge dispersion, not microscopic uniformity.

**framing:** “Spread a teaspoon of the finished mince thinly on the board.”

### 6. Julienne

**Best assessment:** outcome.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Thin, matchstick-like strips.
- Classical professional target is about 1/8 × 1/8 × 2–2.5 in (3 × 3 × 50–60 mm), with straight, consistent cuts. [SRC]
- Knife remains upright enough that sticks do not become triangular wedges.

**acceptableVariations**
- 2 vs 2.5 inches is a school/reference difference; irrelevant to home mastery.
- Natural tapered ends are acceptable unless doing a formal knife-cut drill.
- Ingredients such as peppers may be julienned from naturally curved panels; perfect square cross-sections are unrealistic.
- Mandoline-produced julienne is valid for *food prep* but does not demonstrate chef-knife julienne skill.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `unsafeHand`.
2. `unevenThickness` — “Use the last strip as your visual spacer and match the next one to it.”
3. `bladeLeaning` — “Bring the blade vertical; the sticks are coming out wedge-shaped.”
4. `unstableStack` — “Make a smaller stack. It’s sliding before you cut.”

**tolerance [INST]**
- Professional drill: near 3 mm square.
- Home: most sticks around 2–4 mm thick and similar enough to cook together; length can vary substantially.

**framing:** “Line up six finished sticks side by side and look straight down.”

### 7. Dice an Onion

**Best assessment:** outcome + safety of process.

**targetTechnique [SRC][DISAGREE]**
- Onion is halved and placed cut-side down before fine work.
- Root may be retained as a handle.
- Final dice is reasonably even.
- Either:
  - traditional radial/vertical + optional horizontal + cross cuts, **or**
  - safer no-horizontal method that rotates/reorients onion sections.
- Chef must accept both.

**acceptableVariations**
- Horizontal cuts are **not required** for mastery. Serious Eats documents a safer method that avoids them; traditional culinary teaching still uses them. [SRC]
- Root left intact or nearly intact.
- Radial cuts following onion curvature can produce good dice.
- Peeling one outer flesh layer is fine if tough/damaged; leaving sound first layer is also fine.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `horizontalTowardPalm` — **Obs:** knife travels horizontally toward a bracing palm/fingers. **Say:** “Stop that cut. Move your hand off the blade’s path—or skip the horizontal cuts entirely.”
2. `unstableOnion` — “Put the cut face flat on the board before you keep going.”
3. `rootSeveredEarly` contextual — “Leave the root end attached until the cross-cuts; it will hold the layers together.”
4. `unevenFinalDice` — “Tighten the spacing on the next cuts so the pieces match more closely.”

**safetySignals**
- Knife point driven horizontally toward the supporting hand.
- Onion rolling on curved side during forceful cut.

**framing:** process: “Angle your view so I can see the onion, knife edge, and holding hand.” Outcome: “Spread the dice into one layer.”

### 8. Mince Garlic

**Best assessment:** outcome, with flavor intent acknowledged.

**targetTechnique [SRC][INST]**
- Clove is peeled; root/hard end removed if present.
- Garlic is reduced to small, fairly even pieces without unsafe fingertip work.
- For a normal mince, stop before it becomes wet paste.

**acceptableVariations [SRC]**
- Slice then chop; smash then chop; rock-chop.
- Pressed, grated, knife-pasted garlic are legitimate preparations but *not equivalent*: more cell damage generally creates more pungency. Chef should ask what result is intended rather than rank one universal method.
- A little sticky smearing is normal.

**rankedMistakes**
1. unsafe hand/blade relation.
2. `largeChunks` — “Pull those few big pieces back into the pile and mince just until they match.”
3. `unintendedPaste` contextual — “Stop chopping—you’ve crossed from mince into paste, which will hit much harder in the dish.”

**framing:** “Spread the garlic thin enough that I can see individual pieces.”

### 9. Chop Herbs

**Best assessment:** outcome.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Leafy herbs are gathered and cut with clean slicing motion.
- Result remains visibly leafy/green rather than wet, blackened, or smashed.
- For parsley/cilantro chop: gather, slice, rotate/gather, repeat rather than pounding indefinitely.
- For basil/mint chiffonade: stack, roll, slice thinly if ribbons are the goal. [SRC]

**acceptableVariations**
- Chiffonade only when ribbons are desired; ordinary chopping is equally valid for many dishes.
- Stems: tender cilantro/parsley stems may be deliberately included; woody herb stems generally not.
- Knife motion varies with blade.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `poundedWet` — “Use fewer, cleaner slices; you’re crushing the herbs into the board.”
2. `overworked` — “Stop there. More chopping will bruise them without making the dish better.”
3. `largeStems` contextual — “Pull out the thick woody stems; the tender ones can stay.”

**outcome**
- Professional garnish: clean consistent cut.
- Home: no wet green paste, no repeated huge leaves/stems unless rustic chop intended.

**framing:** “Spread the chopped herbs once and let me see whether they’re still dry and leafy.”

### 10. Slice Against the Grain

**Best assessment:** result + orientation before cutting.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Cook identifies visible muscle-fiber direction.
- Knife cuts across those fibers, ideally near-perpendicular for tougher cuts.
- Slices are appropriate thickness for tenderness and serving.

**acceptableVariations**
- Exact 90° is not required; a bias slice can both cut across grain and create broader slices.
- Tender cuts are less sensitive to grain direction.
- Grain can change direction within a large cut; reorienting the meat is correct.
- Some braised/shredded preparations make this skill irrelevant.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `withGrain` — **Obs:** long fibers run the length of each slice. **Say:** “Turn the meat about a quarter-turn and cut across those long fibers instead.”
2. `grainNotChecked` — “Before the next slice, pause and trace the muscle lines with your eyes.”
3. `tooThickForToughCut` contextual — “Make the slices thinner; this cut will eat more tenderly that way.”

**not visually assessable:** inherent tenderness; ask cut type if unknown.

**framing:** “Before cutting, hold the whole piece in view for two seconds. Then make three slices and show me one cut face.”

### 11. Mirepoix Challenge

**Best assessment:** outcome + cooking stage, not geometric perfection.

**Target [SRC][INST]**
- Classical French reference: roughly 2 parts onion : 1 part carrot : 1 part celery by proportion. [SRC]
- Pieces sized for the intended cook time and roughly consistent.
- If challenge includes cooking: aromatics soften/sweat without unwanted scorching unless browning is deliberately desired.

**acceptableVariations**
- Exact ratio changes by cuisine/dish; “2:1:1” is a useful French baseline, not universal law.
- Rustic stock mirepoix can be large; quick sauce mirepoix needs smaller pieces.
- Celery leaf, leek, or other aromatic substitutions belong to a different aromatic base but can be functionally correct for a recipe.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `sizeMismatchedToCook` — “Cut the largest pieces down; they won’t soften in the same time as the rest.”
2. `ratioWayOff` contextual — “For classical mirepoix, bring the onion up to about half the total mix.”
3. `burningAromatics` if cooked — “Lower the heat and stir now; those edges are going past sweating into burning.”

**Recommendation:** move this challenge after `sauté` if it includes cooking.

## HEAT & PAN CONTROL

### 12. Control Heat on Your Stove (currently “Stove Levels”)

**Best assessment:** dialogue + downstream pan response. Do not visually grade a knob position.

**targetTechnique [INST]**
- Cook knows low/medium/high are *relative heat outputs*, not universal dial numbers.
- Cook adjusts based on what is happening in the pan, not loyalty to the original setting.
- Cook can reduce heat when browning accelerates and increase when food is steaming without browning.

**acceptableVariations**
- Gas, induction, coil, radiant electric all respond differently.
- Large burner/small pan and small burner/large pan change effective heat.
- Heavy cast iron changes slowly; thin aluminum changes quickly.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `settingOverEvidence` — “Ignore the number on the dial and lower the heat—the food is browning faster than you can control.”
2. `noAdjustment` — “Give the pan more heat; it lost its sizzle when the food went in.”
3. `thermalOvershoot` electric/cast iron — “Move the pan off the burner for a moment instead of waiting for the dial to catch up.”

**audio:** sizzle intensity/rate is useful contextual evidence.

**framing:** “Keep looking at the pan, not the knob. I’ll judge the food’s response.”

---

### 13. Preheat a Pan

**Best assessment:** process + ready cue.

**targetTechnique**
- Empty or appropriately oiled pan is heated deliberately to a task-appropriate state before food enters *when that method calls for preheating*.
- Heat is not automatically “as hot as possible.”
- Cast iron gets longer, gentler preheat for evenness. [SRC]

**acceptableVariations [DISAGREE]**
- Many sears/sautés benefit from preheating.
- Cold-start techniques are legitimate for skin-on chicken, fatty duck, bacon, some fish, and certain steak methods; Chef must not enforce preheat when the lesson/recipe intentionally cold-starts. [SRC]
- Nonstick should not be aggressively heated empty for long periods; use lower/moderate heat and follow cookware guidance.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `foodTooEarly` — weak/no initial response where a hot start is intended. “Give the pan another minute before the food goes in.”
2. `overheatedEmpty` — smoking/visible scorching before fat/food. “Take the pan off the heat for a moment; it’s hotter than this job needs.”
3. `castIronHotSpot` — strong center response, cool edge. “Give the cast iron more time at a lower setting so the whole surface catches up.”

**not visually assessable:** actual surface temperature without sensor.

**framing:** “Hold the empty pan in view through the last part of preheating; then show me the cue you use before adding food.”

### 14. Know When a Pan Is Ready

**Best assessment:** cue + immediate food response.

**targetTechnique [INST]**
- Readiness is defined for the next task: sauté, sear, egg, butter, etc.
- Oil should move freely and coat the surface at many sauté/sear temperatures; it need not smoke.
- The first piece of food gives a clear but controllable sizzle for a hot sauté/sear. [SRC/audio]

**acceptableVariations**
- Water-drop tests are optional and can be misleading across cookware/temperatures; never require them.
- Oil “shimmer” is subtle and highly lighting-dependent; do not make it the only criterion.
- For delicate eggs/fish, quieter response may be appropriate.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `coldEntry` — food enters and sits nearly silent/starts weeping. “Take that back out if you can and let the pan recover; we want an immediate gentle-to-strong sizzle for this.”
2. `smokingOil` — “Pull the pan off the heat; the oil is smoking before the food is in.”
3. `testBecomesHazard` — flicking water into hot fat. “Don’t throw water into hot oil—use the oil movement and a small piece of food instead.”

**audio:** high value. Sharp initial sizzle supports readiness for searing; muted response can indicate cold pan or wet/crowded food, so Chef must combine audio with context. [SRC]

### 15. Read Oil in a Pan

**Best assessment:** visual.

**targetTechnique**
- Enough oil to create a thin functional film for sautéing/searing unless food supplies its own fat.
- Oil moves as pan tilts; dry bare patches are corrected.
- Oil is hot enough for task but below active smoking for routine cooking.

**acceptableVariations**
- Amount changes radically with pan coating, ingredient absorption, and technique.
- Mushrooms/eggplant can absorb oil and later release it; adding oil during cook can be correct. [SRC]
- Stainless, cast iron/carbon steel, and nonstick need different amounts.
- High-fat steak may need little/no added oil.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `dryPan` — “Add just enough oil to restore a thin film under the food.”
2. `poolingOil` — “You have more oil than this sauté needs; pour off the excess before it fries.”
3. `smokingOil` — “Lower the heat and let that oil come back below smoking before you cook.”

**safety:** water hitting substantial hot oil; oil igniting; pouring water on oil fire = immediate stop.

### 16. Avoid Crowding

**Best assessment:** pan layout + sound + outcome.

**targetTechnique**
- Food has enough contact with hot pan and enough exposed space that released moisture can evaporate.
- Pieces are not stacked over one another where browning is required.
- Pan maintains active sizzle instead of filling with liquid/steam.

**acceptableVariations**
- “Space between every piece” is not absolute; thin vegetables can overlap briefly during sauté if regularly tossed.
- Braising, wilting greens, sweating onions, and steaming do not follow searing spacing rules.
- Powerful burners/wide pans tolerate more load.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `steamingInsteadOfBrowning` — visible pooled liquid + pale food + collapsed sizzle. “Take some food out and cook in two batches so this moisture can boil off.”
2. `stackedSear` — “Give every piece its own contact with the pan; the ones on top can’t brown.”
3. `needlessUnderfill` — not a mistake; never correct sparse pan unless it causes another issue.

**audio:** loss of sizzle after loading is useful; distinguish temporary temperature drop from sustained wet steaming.

### 17. Sauté

**Best assessment:** process + color/outcome.

**targetTechnique**
- Small/medium pieces cook relatively quickly in a modest film of fat.
- Pan hot enough to cook/brown without prolonged wet simmering.
- Food is moved/tossed/stirred as needed for evenness, but not compulsively if browning requires contact.
- Heat adjusted as load changes.

**acceptableVariations**
- Tossing is not required; spatula/stirring is fine.
- Some sautés seek no color (delicate aromatics), others seek browning.
- Oil, clarified butter, whole butter, or blends depending heat and flavor.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `heatMismatch` — burning or wet steaming. “Lower the heat—the outside is coloring before the inside can catch up.” / “Raise the heat—the food is sitting in its own water.”
2. `crowding`.
3. `constantMovement` contextual — “Leave it against the pan for a moment; you’re moving it faster than it can brown.”
4. `neglectedMovement` contextual — “Turn the pieces now; one face is getting all the heat.”

**framing:** “Show me the pan from above for ten seconds while you cook normally.”

### 18. Sear

**Best assessment:** outcome + sound + temperature plan.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Surface is dry enough and pan conditions support browning.
- Meat/food develops a deep brown crust without charred bitter patches.
- Sear is understood as flavor/color development, **not ‘sealing in juices.’** [SRC]
- Internal doneness is monitored separately.

**acceptableVariations [DISAGREE]**
- Hot-start, frequent-flip, reverse-sear finishing, and some cold-start methods can all produce excellent results.
- Do not require “never move it.” Frequent flipping is legitimate.
- Butter may enter later for basting; high-smoke-point fat may start the sear.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `burningBeforeBrowning` — black spots/smoke while rest is pale. “Lower the heat; we want deep brown, not black, before the center overcooks.”
2. `wetSurface` — visible water/steam, weak browning. “Pat the next surface dry before it hits the pan.”
3. `crowding`.
4. `donenessIgnored` — “Check the center temperature now; crust is not a doneness test.”

**outcome [INST]**
- Good: mostly deep golden-to-brown contact surface, limited black char unless desired.
- Bad: pale/gray wet surface; or bitter black crust with underdeveloped brown.

### 19. Read Butter in a Pan

**Best assessment:** color + foam + audio.

**targetTechnique**
- Cook recognizes melting → foaming/sizzling as water cooks off → milk solids begin coloring → nutty brown stage → burnt black solids.
- Heat is lowered/removed before desired stage overshoots.
- Butter used at temperature appropriate to task.

**acceptableVariations**
- Clarified butter behaves differently: little/no milk-solid browning.
- Cultured/high-water butter foams differently.
- Dark cast iron makes color much harder to judge; spooning butter into a light spoon or using a light pan improves assessment.
- Butter deliberately kept pale for eggs is not “undercooked.”

**rankedMistakes**
1. `burntButter` — black specks, acrid smoke. “Start that butter again; those milk solids are burnt, not browned.”
2. `heatTooHigh` — foam and solids race to brown. “Lower the heat now; butter goes from brown to burnt in seconds.”
3. `wrongStageForTask` contextual — “Add the eggs now; don’t wait for brown butter unless that flavor is intentional.”

**audio:** sizzling quiets as free water decreases; useful secondary signal, never sole doneness signal.

**framing:** “Tilt the pan so the butter pools where I can see its color, or lift a spoonful if the pan is dark.”

### 20. Deglaze

**Best assessment:** process + pan bottom after.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- After browning, excess fat may be removed while flavorful brown fond remains.
- Appropriate liquid is added carefully.
- Cook scrapes/agitate loosened brown fond into liquid over heat.
- Burnt black residue is not treated as valuable fond. [SRC]

**acceptableVariations**
- Water, stock, juice, vinegar mixtures, or recipe-appropriate liquid can deglaze; wine is not mandatory.
- Wooden spoon, silicone spatula, whisk as pan permits.
- Nonstick may produce little fond; not a failure.
- Cast iron can deglaze, but long acidic cooking may affect seasoning; brief deglaze is commonly workable.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `burntFondUsed` — “Those bits are black and bitter—don’t build the sauce on them.”
2. `fondLeftStuck` — “Scrape the brown layer while the liquid is bubbling; that’s the flavor you’re trying to lift.”
3. `tooMuchFat` — oily liquid layer. “Pour off the excess fat first; keep the browned bits.”
4. `unsafeLiquidAddition` — face/hand directly over steam or large cold liquid splashed into very hot fat. “Pull the pan off the heat and add the liquid away from your face.”

**safety:** alcohol poured directly from bottle over flame = stop; use measured amount away from flame.

---

### 21. Reduce

**Best assessment:** visible volume/viscosity + bubble character; flavor still requires tasting.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Liquid simmers/boils at controlled rate to evaporate water and concentrate flavor.
- Cook watches both *volume* and *consistency*, not clock alone.
- Reduction stops before scorching or becoming excessively salty/intense.
- For pan sauce without starch, reduction can create body. [SRC]

**acceptableVariations**
- Wide pan reduces faster; narrow pot slower.
- Stronger boil can be fine for robust water/stock reductions; delicate emulsions/cream need gentler control.
- “Reduce by half” is a volume instruction, not necessarily a fixed texture.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `overReduced` — sticky/oily/syrupy beyond goal. “Add a splash of water or stock and loosen it back to sauce consistency.”
2. `underReduced` — watery, runs like broth when body intended. “Keep it simmering; it should coat more before we stop.”
3. `scorchingEdges` — “Lower the heat and scrape the edge of the reduction back in.”
4. `seasonedTooEarly` contextual — if very salty after reduction. “Reduce first next time, then do the final salt adjustment.”

**not visually assessable:** salt/acid concentration. Ask: “Taste a cooled drop—did the flavor get too salty or sharp as it reduced?”

---

### 22. Pan Sauce Challenge

**Best assessment:** process + final emulsion/body.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
1. Usable golden/brown fond, not burnt.
2. Excess fat managed.
3. Aromatic optional.
4. Deglaze and scrape.
5. Reduce to flavor/body.
6. Optional stock/cream; reduce appropriately.
7. Finish off/lower heat with butter/emulsifier as recipe calls.
8. Taste and balance.

**acceptableVariations**
- Endless flavor paths; wine not required.
- Butter finish optional.
- Cream-based vs stock-based vs water-based are all valid.
- Gelatin-rich stock naturally gives stronger body.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `burntFond`.
2. `waterySauce`.
3. `brokenGreasyFinish` — “Take it off the heat and whisk in a teaspoon of water; the fat is separating.”
4. `overReducedSalty`.
5. `fondNotReleased`.

**result**
- Sauce should be cohesive enough to cling lightly to food/spoon, not leave a separate greasy puddle unless style dictates.
- Taste cannot be certified by camera.

## COOKING BASICS

### 23. Read a Recipe

**Assessability:** **not a camera mastery skill.** This should be a guided interaction/checklist, not a vision rubric.

**target [INST]**
- Read title/yield, ingredient list, and all steps before starting.
- Identify time traps: marinating, preheating, chilling, resting, “divided” ingredients.
- Identify equipment and dependencies.
- Distinguish prep quantities (“1 onion, diced”) from cooking actions.

**mistakes to coach**
1. `startedBeforeReading` — “Before you heat anything, scan the whole method once.”
2. `hiddenWaitMissed` — “There’s a 30-minute rest later; plan that before you start.”
3. `ingredientDividedMissed` — “That butter is used in two places—don’t add it all now.”

**visual use:** Chef can confirm mise en place and equipment but should not claim “recipe reading mastery” from sight.

---

### 24. Mise en Place

**Best assessment:** scene/layout + recipe context.

**target [SRC][INST]**
- Necessary ingredients/tools are identified and reasonably ready before a time-sensitive cook begins.
- High-risk time-critical items are prepped before heat.
- Raw/cooked cross-contamination is controlled.
- Workspace has a clear landing area.

**acceptableVariations**
- “Everything in tiny bowls” is **not required**. Pros often stage efficiently; home cooks can prep as they go when timing permits.
- Long-cooking components can start before every garnish is chopped.
- Minimalist mise is fine if sequencing is safe.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `missingCriticalPrep` — pan already hot while essential ingredient still unopened/uncut. “Turn the heat down and finish that prep before the pan gets away from you.”
2. `rawCrossContamination` — “Move the ready-to-eat food away from the raw-meat board and use a clean surface.”
3. `noLandingZone` — “Clear one dry spot for the hot pan/finished food before you continue.”

**safety:** raw meat juices visibly contacting ready-to-eat foods = intervene.

---

### 25. Season as You Go

**Assessability:** mostly **dialogue/taste**, with process events visible.

**target [SRC]**
- Season incrementally at meaningful stages, especially components that will not later absorb seasoning.
- Final seasoning is adjusted after reductions/concentration.
- Avoid adding full final salt early to a sauce that will reduce substantially. [SRC]

**acceptableVariations**
- Some foods are intentionally salted well in advance (dry brining); others at service.
- Salt timing differs by ingredient and desired texture.
- “Season every single step” is not literal.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `allSaltAtEnd` contextual — “Season this component now so the flavor is inside it, not just on the finished plate.”
2. `fullSaltBeforeReduction` — “Hold the rest of the salt until after this reduces.”
3. `blindSeasoning` — “Taste before you add more.”

**Mastery:** cannot be visually certified; Chef needs spoken taste report.

---

### 26. Taste as You Go

**Assessability:** camera can verify tasting behavior, **not palate accuracy**.

**target**
- Taste at safe, useful checkpoints.
- Use a clean spoon and avoid cross-contamination.
- Cool very hot liquids before tasting.
- Compare what changed after an adjustment.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `neverTastes` — “Before you add anything else, taste it.”
2. `adjustsBeforeTaste` — “Don’t chase the seasoning blind—taste first.”
3. `unsafeTaste` — raw meat/egg mixture or boiling-hot spoon. “Don’t taste that yet; it isn’t safe / let the spoonful cool first.”
4. `doubleDip` hygiene — “Use a clean spoon for the next taste.”

**No visual pass/fail for ‘correctly seasoned.’**

---

### 27. Simmer vs Boil

**Best assessment:** vision + audio. Strong skill for Glutt.

**targetTechnique**
- **Simmer:** gentle, intermittent-to-steady small bubbles, surface movement controlled.
- **Boil:** vigorous, continuous bubbling across much of the surface.
- Cook chooses based on task and adjusts heat to maintain state.

**acceptableVariations**
- “Simmer” spans bare simmer to active simmer; exact bubble rate is recipe-dependent.
- Starchy liquids foam; sauce viscosity changes bubble size.
- Covered pot can appear more active after lid removal.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `boilingWhenSimmerNeeded` — “Lower the heat until the surface is gently bubbling instead of rolling.”
2. `notActuallySimmering` — “Raise it slightly; I want regular small bubbles, not just hot still liquid.”
3. `foamOverRisk` — “Lower the heat and move the pot if needed—it’s climbing toward the rim.”

**audio:** boil has dense continuous bubbling; simmer is more intermittent. Combine with sight.

---

### 28. Judge Doneness

**Recommendation:** split into food families later. Current skill should teach *choosing the right signal*, not guessing a universal visual.

**target [INST]**
- Protein safety/doneness: thermometer when practical.
- Vegetables: resistance/texture + color depending goal.
- Cakes/breads: internal structure/temperature/visual cues depending item.
- Sauces: consistency + taste.
- Eggs: visible set + desired style, with safety caveats.

**critical rule [SRC]**
- **Color alone does not establish meat/poultry safety.** Use thermometer. [SRC]
- Chef must never say raw poultry is safe from camera color.

**rankedMistake**
1. `colorAsSafety` — “Don’t use the color to call that safe—check the thickest part with the thermometer.”
2. `clockAsProof` — “The timer tells us when to check, not whether it’s done.”
3. `singleCue` — “Use the cue that actually matters for this food.”

**Assessability:** teachable; not certifiable as one monolithic visual skill.

---

### 29. Use a Thermometer

**Best assessment:** process, highly watchable.

**targetTechnique [SRC]**
- Probe enters the thickest/thermal-center region appropriate to food.
- Avoid obvious bone, pan, or huge fat pocket when measuring meat.
- For thin food, insert from the side when possible to get probe tip centered.
- Check more than one spot when geometry is irregular.
- Read stable lowest/relevant temperature, then clean probe.

**acceptableVariations**
- Instant-read vs leave-in probe.
- Angle/entry point varies.
- Different pull temperatures depending recipe + carryover, while safety minima remain non-negotiable for consumer guidance.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `probeTooShallow` — “Push the sensing tip into the center of the thickest part.”
2. `touchingBoneOrPan` — “Back the probe off the bone/pan and measure the meat itself.”
3. `oneRandomSpot` — “Check one more nearby spot—I want the coolest part, not the first number.”
4. `dirtyProbeReuse` — “Clean the probe before it touches cooked food again.”

**Safety references [SRC]:** whole beef/pork/lamb/veal 145°F/63°C + 3-min rest; ground meat 160°F/71°C; poultry 165°F/74°C; egg dishes 160°F/71°C; fish 145°F/63°C for consumer safety guidance. Recipe-specific quality targets can differ but must not be presented as superseding safety guidance without an appropriate time-temperature framework.

---

### 30. Rest Food

**Best assessment:** process/time + temperature context, not camera appearance.

**target**
- Recognize carryover cooking after removal from heat.
- Rest larger meats long enough for temperature gradients/pressure to settle before slicing.
- Preserve crust by avoiding unnecessary tight covering/steam.
- Small cuts may need only service transit; do not impose arbitrary “10 minutes for every steak.” [SRC][DISAGREE]

**acceptableVariations**
- Rest duration depends on size, cooking method, desired serving temperature, and crust.
- Some reverse-sear workflows rest *before* the final sear.
- Crispy foods may prioritize immediate service.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `cutsLargeRoastImmediately` — “Give the roast time before slicing; it’s still carrying heat into the center.”
2. `ignoresCarryover` — “Pull it before the final target; the center will keep rising while it rests.”
3. `steamsCrust` — “Leave the crisp surface uncovered or tent loosely—tight foil will soften it.”

**Do not teach the simplistic claim that meat ‘reabsorbs all its juices.’** Pressure/temperature redistribution and carryover are safer explanations; sources disagree on the old phrasing. [DISAGREE]

---

### 31. Cook Without a Timer Challenge

**Best assessment:** process + multiple sensory checks.

**target [INST]**
- Student uses timer only as a reminder if desired, not as proof of doneness.
- Names and checks at least two appropriate cues: color, bubble rate, texture/probe resistance, internal temperature, aroma (self-reported), sound.
- Adjusts heat/time from observed progress.

**acceptableVariations**
- A timer is still a professional tool. “Without a timer” should mean *not blindly time-driven*, not anti-timer.
- Baking remains more time/temperature constrained; do not generalize stovetop intuition to precise pastry.

**rankedMistakes**
1. `guessingInsteadOfChecking` — “Don’t guess—pick the cue that tells you whether this food is actually done.”
2. `oneCueOnly` — “Check one more signal before you call it.”
3. `visualProteinSafety` — thermometer required.

# 2. UNWRITTEN SKILLS — LESSON + RUBRIC

## EGGS

### 32. Crack an Egg

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Crack cleanly with minimal shell and keep broken shell away from the food.
- **steps:** (1) Tap the egg once or twice on a flat work surface. (2) Put both thumbs into the fracture and pull the shell halves apart over a bowl. (3) Check for shell before combining with other ingredients.
- **watchFors:** crushing with the thumbs; shell fragments; cracking directly into a large finished mixture when separation matters.
- **whyItMatters:** a clean opening keeps shell out and makes yolk control easier. Jacques Pépin/ICE specifically recommends a flat surface. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- **better:** process.
- **target:** controlled fracture near midline; shell opened without thumb plunging through yolk.
- **variations:** bowl-edge cracking is widely used by competent cooks; because the teaching target is cleaner shell control, treat it as *less preferred for this lesson*, not dangerous/wrong. [DISAGREE]
- **mistakes:** `crushedShell` → “Use a lighter tap, then open the crack with your thumbs instead of squeezing the egg.” `thumbThroughEgg` → “Put your thumbs into the crack and pull the shell apart—don’t push them down into the egg.”
- **safety:** raw egg contamination on ready-to-eat surface; prompt cleanup/handwashing.
- **framing:** “Crack one egg slowly where I can see your hands and the bowl.”

### 33. Scrambled Eggs

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Control coagulation so the eggs set into the texture you actually want instead of drying out.
- **steps:** (1) Beat eggs until reasonably uniform; season according to preferred method. (2) Cook in butter/fat over controlled low-to-medium heat, moving the set egg back into the liquid. (3) Pull while slightly softer than the target because residual heat continues cooking.
- **watchFors:** browned/dry curds when soft eggs intended; raw liquid left for a fully-cooked style; heat too high.
- **whyItMatters:** scrambled eggs are mostly heat control; texture can move from creamy to dry in seconds.

**Rubric**
- **target:** curd size and final wetness match stated style.
- **variations [DISAGREE]:** French soft/custardy, American fluffy/large-curd, firmer diner-style are all legitimate. Pre-salting is not inherently wrong; controlled tests support it and dispute the common “salt makes eggs tough” rule. [SRC]
- **mistakes ranked:** `heatTooHigh` → “Lower the heat—the eggs are setting faster than you can move them.” `overcooked` → “Take them off now; the pan will finish the last bit.” `unevenRawWhite` → “Fold the liquid into the set curds so there aren’t raw pockets.”
- **safety:** for conservative food-safety standard, no visible liquid egg; high-risk users should use fully cooked/pasteurized-egg guidance. [SRC]
- **audio:** quiet gentle sizzle for tender styles; loud frying is evidence of hotter pan, not an automatic failure if browned style intended.
- **framing:** “Keep the pan in view for the last 30 seconds; tell me whether you want creamy or fluffy before I judge it.”

### 34. Fried Egg

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Set the white while controlling yolk and edge texture.
- **steps:** (1) Heat fat at a temperature suited to tender or crisp style. (2) Add egg gently. (3) Cook, baste, cover, or flip until the white is set to the desired style.
- **watchFors:** raw gelatinous white around yolk; burnt underside; broken yolk when not intended.
- **why:** the white and yolk set at different rates, so heat and top-side cooking method matter.

**Rubric**
- **variations [DISAGREE]:** crisp-edged high-heat fried egg and tender low-heat sunny-side egg are both correct; over-easy/medium/hard, basted, covered/steamed all valid.
- **mistakes:** `rawWhite` → “Give the top white more heat—cover it briefly or baste around the yolk.” `burntBottom` → “Lower the heat; the bottom is burning before the white finishes.” `yolkBroken` contextual → “Slide the spatula under farther before you turn it so the yolk isn’t taking the bend.”
- **safety:** runny-yolk styles carry more egg-safety risk; do not present them as equivalent to fully cooked guidance for high-risk eaters. [SRC]
- **outcome:** judge chosen style, not one universal “perfect egg.”

### 35. Soft-Boiled Egg

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Produce a set white with a warm fluid-to-jammy yolk using repeatable water/egg timing.
- **steps:** (1) Bring chosen water method to a known state. (2) Lower egg gently and begin timing at the method’s defined point. (3) Cool promptly when target is reached.
- **watchFors:** cracked egg from dropping; uncontrolled rolling boil; no cooling when exact yolk state matters.
- **why:** the center keeps cooking after removal unless cooled.

**Rubric**
- **best assessment:** **result** after cutting, not pot motion.
- **variations [DISAGREE]:** cold-start, boiling-water start, steaming are all legitimate; times differ with egg size, starting temp, altitude, and method.
- **target:** white set; yolk state matches stated goal.
- **mistakes:** `overcookedYolk` result → “Cool the next egg sooner; this one carried past the jammy stage.” `unsetWhite` → “Give the next one more cooking time before cooling.”
- **visual limitation:** cannot see inside intact shell. This skill cannot be certified until egg is opened.

### 36. Hard-Boiled Egg

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Fully set the egg without unnecessarily rubbery white or gray-green yolk surface.
- **steps:** use a controlled boil/steam method; cook to full set; cool.
- **watchFors:** violent boil cracking shells; prolonged high heat; overcooking.
- **why:** excess heat toughens white and promotes gray-green iron-sulfur discoloration around yolk.

**Rubric**
- **best:** cut result.
- **variations:** boil, steam, pressure methods can all work.
- **target:** fully set yolk, tender white, minimal gray-green ring.
- **mistake:** `greenRing` → “Shorten the cook or cool faster next time; that ring is an overcooking cue, not a safety requirement.”
- **safety:** fully cooked egg is the conservative standard. [SRC]

### 37. Poached Egg

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Set a compact white around a soft yolk in gently simmering water.
- **steps:** (1) Use fresh egg when possible; crack into a small cup. (2) Keep water at gentle simmer, optionally lightly acidulated. (3) Lower egg close to water and cook until white is set to desired style; lift and drain.
- **watchFors:** rolling boil shredding white; dropping from height; fragile old white spreading.
- **why:** gentle water lets white set around yolk instead of tearing apart.

**Rubric**
- **variations [DISAGREE]:** vortex useful for a single egg but not required; vinegar optional; straining watery white is optional; shape need not be a perfect restaurant orb.
- **target:** majority of white coheres around yolk; no large translucent raw-white patches for standard service.
- **mistakes:** `boilingHard` → “Lower the water to a gentle simmer; the boil is tearing the white apart.” `dropHigh` → “Bring the cup right to the water before you slide it in.”
- **safety:** soft yolk is not fully-cooked egg guidance; flag for high-risk populations. [SRC]
- **framing:** pan overhead + final egg on slotted spoon.

### 38. Omelette

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Set beaten eggs into a cohesive omelette without drying them, then fold/roll in the style intended.
- **steps:** (1) Beat evenly and heat butter. (2) Add eggs and redistribute uncooked egg as the base sets. (3) Add modest filling if used and fold/roll before dry.
- **watchFors:** browned exterior when French omelette intended; overloaded filling; tearing from waiting too long.
- **why:** an omelette is controlled coagulation plus timing.

**Rubric**
- **variations [DISAGREE]:** French pale, smooth, soft-centered omelette; American browned/fluffy folded omelet; country styles are all legitimate. **Chef must ask the style first.**
- **ranked mistakes:** `styleHeatMismatch` → “Lower the heat; for a French omelette I don’t want the outside browning.” `overfilled` → “Take some filling out; the egg sheet can’t close around that much.” `overSetBeforeFold` → “Fold now while the surface still has some flexibility.”
- **outcome:** intact enough to serve, texture matches style; cosmetic seams are minor.

### 39. Egg Mastery Challenge

**Recommended challenge:** three eggs, three heat-control targets rather than trivia.
1. Soft scrambled eggs in stated style.
2. Fried egg in stated style.
3. Omelette or poached egg chosen by user.
- Must verbalize heat adjustment and doneness cue.
- Pass requires repeatability: two of three techniques performed correctly twice on separate sessions; safety issues reset the attempt. [INST]

## MEAT

### 40. Season Meat

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Salt meat in a way that seasons the interior/surface without sabotaging browning.
- **steps:** dry surface; salt evenly; either cook soon after salting or give enough lead time for dry brining; add heat-sensitive spices according to recipe.
- **watchFors:** patchy salt; thick clumps; sugary rub burned during hard sear.
- **why:** salt amount/distribution and timing affect both flavor and surface moisture.

**Rubric**
- **best:** process; taste cannot be visually certified.
- **variations:** immediate salting and advance dry-brining both valid; exact grams depend food thickness and recipe.
- **mistakes:** `patchySeasoning` → “Season from a little higher and cover the whole surface, including the edges.” `wetSaltWindow` contextual → “If the salt has pulled moisture to the surface, either give it more time or pat it dry before the sear.”
- **not visual:** actual saltiness. Ask after cooking.

### 41. Dry Before Searing

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Remove free surface moisture so pan energy can brown the food instead of first boiling off water.
- **steps:** blot all exposed surfaces; inspect creases/skin; season appropriately; sear.
- **watchFors:** glossy water film; marinade dripping; paper towel sticking excessively to delicate fish.
- **why:** free water delays surface temperature rise and browning. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- **target:** surface looks matte rather than wet.
- **variations:** dry-brined meat can already be dry; some marinades intentionally remain but will change sear; skin-on poultry needs especially dry skin for crisping.
- **mistake:** `visibleWetSurface` → “Blot that surface until it looks matte before it hits the pan.”
- **framing:** close look at surface before and immediately after blotting.

### 42. Internal Temperature

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Treat doneness and safety as temperature problems, not color guesses.
- **steps:** know target/safety floor; probe thermal center correctly; account for carryover and verify multiple spots where needed.
- **watchFors:** surface-color guessing; probe touching bone/pan; pulling exactly at final target for large hot roast without carryover allowance.
- **why:** time and appearance are unreliable; temperature is measurable. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- same core process as Thermometer skill.
- **consumer safety floors [SRC]:** whole beef/pork/lamb/veal 145°F + 3 min; ground meat 160°F; poultry 165°F.
- **quality targets:** may differ by cut/style; app must distinguish “chef doneness target” from “regulatory consumer safety minimum.”

### 43. Sear Meat

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build flavorful brown crust while protecting internal doneness.
- **steps:** dry/season; establish appropriate pan heat/fat; sear and turn as needed; temperature-check separately.
- **watchFors:** pale wet surface; black scorching; crowding; chasing crust after center is already done.
- **why:** sear creates Maillard flavor; it does not seal juices. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- same visual logic as Sear skill, with thermometer dependency.
- frequent flipping is allowed.
- cold-start techniques are allowed where explicitly chosen; do not universalize ripping-hot cast iron. [DISAGREE]

### 44. Baste a Steak

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Use foaming butter/fat to flavor and heat the steak’s upper surface without burning the butter or overshooting the center.
- **steps:** establish sear; lower/adjust heat and add butter/aromatics; tilt pan safely; spoon foaming butter over steak while monitoring temperature.
- **watchFors:** black butter solids; pan tilted toward body; basting so long steak overcooks.
- **why:** basting adds aromatic browned-butter flavor and top-side heat, but trades against doneness margin. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- **target:** butter foamy/golden-to-brown, spoon path controlled, steak temp monitored.
- **variations:** aromatics optional; number/frequency of bastes not fixed; some steak methods omit basting entirely.
- **mistakes:** `burntBastingButter` → “Lower the heat and replace the butter if it’s black; bitter butter won’t improve the steak.” `unsafeTilt` → “Tilt the handle away and keep the butter pooled on the far side, not toward your body.” `bastePastTarget` → “Stop basting and check the center temperature now.”
- **safety:** pan fat near spill edge = stop.

### 45. Rest Meat

**Lesson**
- See written Rest Food skill, but focus on carryover, size, and slicing.
- **why:** carryover can be several degrees for small cuts and much larger for large roasts depending method. [SRC]
- Avoid fake universal rules like “rest exactly half the cook time.”

**Rubric**
- **target:** student predicts carryover, gives larger roast meaningful rest, protects desired crust.
- **mistake:** slicing a large roast immediately; tenting crispy skin tightly.
- **not visual:** internal pressure/temperature equalization; thermometer trace is better.

### 46. Butterfly a Chicken Breast

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Open a thick breast into a thinner, more even sheet without cutting through into two pieces unless that is intended.
- **steps:** place breast flat and stable; hold top with palm well above blade path; slice horizontally through thick side, stopping before far edge; open like a book and even thickness if needed.
- **watchFors:** knife aimed toward palm; sawing blindly; cutting completely through.
- **why:** even thickness cooks more evenly and is useful for stuffing/cutlets.

**Rubric**
- **target:** connected hinge remains; opened breast has reasonably even thickness.
- **variations:** slicing fully into two cutlets is a valid *different task*; pounding after butterflying optional.
- **mistakes:** `bladeTowardPalm` **safety #1** → “Stop. Move your top hand so the knife is never traveling toward your palm.” `cutThroughHinge` → “Stop about a centimeter before the far edge so it opens like a book.” `unevenThickness` → “Angle the knife toward the thickest area and level it out.”
- **safety:** this is a skill I would **not** auto-run without clear view of both blade direction and support hand.

### 47. Cook a Great Steak Challenge

**Lesson/challenge**
- Dry/season intentionally.
- Choose a sear strategy suited to thickness/equipment.
- Build brown crust without bitter char.
- Measure center temperature.
- Pull for carryover.
- Rest appropriately.
- Slice against grain when serving style benefits.

**Pass [INST]:**
- no safety error;
- crust mostly brown, not pale or burnt;
- final center within roughly ±5°F (±3°C) of the user’s stated quality target for an ordinary steak, **while still respecting food-safety framing**;
- student can explain one heat adjustment they made based on what they saw/heard.

## SAUCES

### 48. What Is Fond?

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Learn to distinguish flavorful browned deposits from burnt residue.
- **steps:** observe pan after browning; identify golden-to-deep-brown adhered bits; distinguish black/acrid burn; use fond by deglazing.
- **watchFors:** washing away good fond; treating black carbon as fond.
- **why:** fond is concentrated browned flavor and the base of many pan sauces. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- **target:** visually identifies brown vs black residue correctly.
- **variations:** stainless shows it best; cast iron hides contrast; nonstick develops less.
- **mistake:** `burntAsFond` → “That part is black, not brown—don’t build the sauce on it.”
- **not visual:** bitterness; ask/taste if uncertain.

### 49. Pan Sauce

Use the Pan Sauce Challenge rubric above as the assessment rubric.

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Turn the browned residue from cooking into a quick sauce.
- **steps:** manage fat/aromatics; deglaze and scrape; reduce; finish/emulsify; taste and balance.
- **watchFors:** burnt fond; too much liquid; broken greasy finish.
- **why:** one pan teaches fond, reduction, emulsification, and seasoning at once. [SRC]

### 50. Vinaigrette

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Balance fat and acid, then disperse them into a temporary or stabilized emulsion.
- **steps:** combine acid + seasoning/emulsifier; whisk while slowly adding oil; taste and adjust.
- **watchFors:** blindly obeying a ratio after taste says otherwise; oil dumped at once for a stable emulsion; overdressing greens.
- **why:** it teaches both flavor balance and emulsion mechanics.

**Rubric**
- **target:** homogeneous immediately after whisking for a basic emulsion; stable enough for intended service if mustard/emulsifier used.
- **ratio:** classical starting point **3 parts oil : 1 part acid** is taught by ICE, but it is a starting ratio, not a law; acid strength and cuisine vary. [SRC][DISAGREE]
- **variations:** 2:1 or sharper dressings valid; shaken jar valid; intentionally broken vinaigrette valid.
- **mistakes:** `oilDumped` contextual → “Add the oil in a thinner stream while you whisk so the droplets stay dispersed.” `flatBalance` not visual → taste dialogue only.
- **not visual:** flavor balance. Ask: “Taste a leaf, not just the dressing—does it need more acid, salt, or oil?”

### 51. Understand an Emulsion

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Learn that two normally separating phases can be dispersed by shear and often stabilized by an emulsifier.
- **steps:** identify fat/water phases; create small droplets by whisking/blending; stabilize where needed; recognize and rescue separation.
- **watchFors:** adding fat faster than emulsion can accept; overheating egg/butter emulsions; confusing thickness with stability.
- **why:** vinaigrette, mayonnaise, hollandaise, beurre blanc, and many pan sauces are variations on the same idea. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- **target:** can make one cold emulsion and identify visible break (greasy layer/droplets).
- **mistake:** `broken` → “Stop adding fat and rebuild the emulsion with a little water/acid, then add the broken sauce back slowly.”
- **variations:** temporary separation can be intentional in vinaigrette; not all emulsions need permanent stability.

### 52. Thicken a Sauce

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Choose the thickening mechanism that suits the sauce instead of adding random flour.
- **steps:** choose reduction / roux / starch slurry / puree / emulsion; apply correctly; simmer/cook as mechanism requires; judge final coating/body.
- **watchFors:** raw flour lumps; cornstarch added dry; over-reduction; confusing cold thickness with hot.
- **why:** thickness should come from a controlled mechanism.

**Rubric**
- **best:** outcome + process.
- **target:** smooth, intentional consistency; for many classic sauces, light spoon coating/nappe is a useful cue, not a universal rule.
- **mistakes:** `lumpyRouxSauce` → “Stop adding liquid and whisk the lumps smooth before you add more.” `rawSlurry` → “Bring the sauce back to a simmer so the starch can thicken.” `tooThick` → “Whisk in a little compatible liquid until it flows again.”
- **equipment:** starches, dairy, acidic sauces behave differently; no one universal viscosity.

### 53. Balance a Sauce

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Diagnose what the sauce is missing before adding more salt.
- **steps:** taste safely; name dominant problem (flat, too rich, too sharp, too salty, bitter); make one small adjustment; taste again.
- **watchFors:** reflex salt; multiple fixes at once; balancing a spoonful rather than sauce with its food.
- **why:** good sauce is relationship, not maximum intensity.

**Rubric**
- **cannot be visually certified.**
- Camera can confirm disciplined process; microphone captures spoken diagnosis.
- **mistakes:** `blindSalt` → “Taste first—flat does not always mean unsalted.” `multiAdjustment` → “Change one thing at a time so you know what fixed it.”
- **Mastery:** verbal diagnosis + successful before/after adjustment across multiple dishes.

---

## FLAVOR & SEASONING

### 54. Salt Properly

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Use salt deliberately for evenness, timing, and concentration.
- **steps:** use consistent salt type/measure; distribute evenly; add incrementally where appropriate; taste before final adjustment.
- **watchFors:** clumps/patches; final salting before big reduction; no tasting.
- **why:** salt changes how other flavors are perceived; it is not merely a salty flavor. [SRC]

**Rubric**
- visual only for distribution/process.
- **variations:** finger pinch height/style irrelevant; measured salt valid; early dry brine vs late seasoning context-dependent.
- **cannot certify saltiness by sight.**

### 55. Understand Acid

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Use acid to brighten and balance richness/salt/sweetness without making food simply sour.
- **steps:** taste; add small amount of appropriate acid; mix; taste with the full dish.
- **watchFors:** large unmeasured pour; using acid to fix a problem that is actually salt/bitterness; adding delicate fresh acid too early when brightness is wanted.
- **why:** acidity can make flavors feel clearer and less heavy.

**Rubric:** dialogue/taste, not visual. Accept citrus, vinegar, fermented acids, tomatoes, wine reductions according to dish.

### 56. Understand Fat

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Treat fat as cooking medium, texture, aroma carrier, and richness—not a generic ingredient to maximize.
- **steps:** identify role; choose fat for heat/flavor; add enough for function; balance richness.
- **watchFors:** smoking low-heat fat; greasy broken sauce; too little fat for sauté contact.
- **rubric:** process/outcome, but flavor effect not visual.

### 57. Understand Umami

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build savoriness through ingredients and browning/fermentation/concentration, not by chasing a mysterious “umami powder.”
- **steps:** identify existing savory base; deepen with browning/stock/aged or fermented ingredient where appropriate; rebalance salt/acid.
- **why:** ICE’s current instruction frames umami as one part of overall balance and technique, not a magic ingredient. [SRC]

**Rubric:** not visually certifiable. Browning can be observed; perceived umami cannot.

### 58. Fix Bland Food

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Diagnose blandness before fixing it.
- **steps:** taste; check salt first but not automatically; check acid/aroma/umami/concentration; make one small change; retaste.
- **watchFors:** adding several seasonings at once; more salt when food is already salty but flat.
- **rubric:** conversation-based.
- **Instructor decision tree:** bland + not salty → small salt; salty enough but dull/heavy → acid/aroma; watery → concentration; savory base weak → umami/browning/stock.

### 59. Fix Oversalted Food

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Reduce the salt concentration or rebalance the dish; do not teach myths like “a potato sucks out the salt.”
- **steps:** stop reduction; add unsalted bulk/liquid where structurally possible; split batch/add unsalted base; rebalance acid/fat/sweetness only as secondary masking.
- **watchFors:** more reduction; random raw potato fix; excessive sugar.
- **rubric:** outcome by taste only; camera can verify chosen rescue mechanism.
- **Correction:** “Don’t try to hide the salt first—dilute the sauce with unsalted base, then rebuild the balance.”

### 60. Finish With Acid

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Add a small fresh acidic note near the end when a dish needs lift.
- **steps:** taste; add a small amount; mix; taste with the food.
- **watchFors:** adding acid automatically to every dish; huge squeeze; boiling away fresh citrus character.
- **rubric:** not visually certifiable; can certify the disciplined taste-adjust-retaste loop.

## COOKING INTUITION

### 61. Know Why Food Tastes Bland

This overlaps heavily with `Fix Bland Food`. **Recommendation: merge them.**
- “Know why” = diagnosis lesson.
- “Fix bland” = challenge using diagnosis.
Keeping both as separate linear skills adds duplication more than mastery.

### 62. Make Substitutions

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Substitute by *function*, not by ingredient resemblance.
- **steps:** name what ingredient does (fat, acid, structure, water, leavening, thickening, aroma); choose replacement serving same critical function; adjust quantity/process; evaluate.
- **watchFors:** substitution that changes food-safety requirement; replacing a structural baking ingredient casually; swapping acid without adjusting concentration.
- **rubric:** primarily reasoning/dialogue, not vision.
- **Camera use:** can identify available pantry candidates, but should not “certify” substitution skill from one success.

### 63. Recover From Overcooking

**Lesson**
- **summary:** First stop the heat; then decide whether the damage is reversible, maskable, or needs repurposing.
- **steps:** remove from heat/hot pan; assess dryness/burn/texture; choose rescue: add compatible moisture/fat/sauce, slice thinner, shred/repurpose, trim burnt area; do not keep cooking.
- **watchFors:** leaving food in hot pan while discussing rescue; trying to re-moisten severely denatured lean meat by more cooking.
- **rubric:** outcome/context; not every overcook is recoverable.
- **Correction:** “Get it out of the hot pan first; we can decide how to save it once it stops cooking.”

### 64. Time Multiple Components

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Work backward from service and start the least flexible/longest components first.
- **steps:** identify finish times + holding tolerance; work backward; stage mise; use resting/holding windows; re-check as cooks run fast/slow.
- **watchFors:** all burners started simultaneously; delicate item finishes first and dies while long component cooks; no resting window.
- **rubric:** workflow/logical rather than visual technique.
- **Mastery challenge:** two hot components + one sauce arrive within a 3–5 minute serving window without one being obviously degraded. [INST]

### 65. Scale a Recipe

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Scale quantities mathematically, then identify things that do *not* scale linearly.
- **steps:** compute factor; scale core ingredients; reconsider pan size, surface area, cook time, salt/spice/leavening where sensitive; batch if needed.
- **watchFors:** doubling pan load without changing cookware; doubling cooking time automatically; doubling salt blindly in a reduction-heavy dish.
- **rubric:** reasoning + outcome, not visually assessable as a body skill.

### 66. Cook Without a Recipe

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build a dish from a cooking method + ingredient functions + doneness/balance cues.
- **steps:** choose main ingredient and method; choose aromatic/fat/seasoning structure; plan doneness cue; cook and adjust; finish/balance.
- **watchFors:** starting heat before plan; changing five variables at once; no doneness check.
- **rubric:** challenge, not single visual skill.
- **Mastery [INST]:** execute two different simple meals from available ingredients while explaining method, doneness cue, and one adjustment made from sensory feedback.

# 3. MOTHER SAUCES — WHAT I WOULD ACTUALLY TEACH IN 2026

## Recommendation: keep the classical five, but do **not** make “Mother Sauces” the whole sauce curriculum

The classical five remain actively taught by ICE, Escoffier, Rouxbe, and other current culinary programs: **béchamel, velouté, espagnole, hollandaise, tomate**. [SRC] They are useful because they expose distinct structures and derivatives.

But for a home-cook skills map, I would call the category **Sauce Foundations** and structure it this way:

### Foundations first
1. **Roux** — starch thickening + color control.
2. **Reduction** — concentration + viscosity.
3. **Cold emulsion** — vinaigrette → mayonnaise.
4. **Warm emulsion** — hollandaise.
5. **Butter emulsion** — beurre blanc / monte au beurre.
6. **Fond + deglaze** — pan sauce.

### Classical applications second
7. Béchamel
8. Velouté
9. Espagnole / demi-glace
10. Sauce tomate
11. Hollandaise

### Why I would alter the canon for Glutt
- **Keep Espagnole**, but teach it *with demi-glace* and explain that demi-glace is more operationally useful in many modern kitchens.
- **Add mayonnaise** as the clear cold-emulsion challenge.
- **Add beurre blanc** as the butter-emulsion challenge.
- **Keep pan sauce** because it is vastly more useful to a home cook and already combines fond, deglazing, reduction, and finishing.
- Do **not** promote mayonnaise or beurre blanc as if they replaced an official classical mother sauce. Call them modern foundational sauces.
- This mirrors current teaching: classical five remain in curricula, while Rouxbe/ICE also teach butter sauces, mayonnaise/emulsion, demi-glace, pan sauces, and contemporary sauces alongside them. [SRC]

## Foundation A: Roux

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Cook equal parts fat and flour into a smooth thickening base, controlling color from white → blond → brown.
- **steps:** melt fat; whisk/stir in flour; cook while stirring to desired color/aroma; use in compatible liquid with controlled incorporation.
- **watchFors:** dry raw-flour pockets; scorched specks; assuming darker roux thickens as strongly as pale roux.
- **why:** roux teaches both starch behavior and color control.

**Ratio**
- Professional baseline: **equal parts fat and flour by weight**. Volume “equal parts” is less precise because flour density varies. [SRC]
- For a home lesson, teach weight first; provide recipe-specific spoon measures second.

**visual stages [INST]**
- **white/pale:** off-white to pale cream, no raw-flour look, little/no toast color.
- **blond:** light tan/golden, color of pale toasted cereal.
- **brown:** peanut-butter/tan through deeper brown depending cuisine; nutty aroma (must be self-reported).
- **burnt:** black flecks, acrid smoke—discard.

**mistakes**
1. `scorchedRoux` → “Those black flecks are burnt; start again before they flavor the whole sauce.”
2. `rawDryPockets` → “Keep stirring until every bit of flour is coated in fat.”
3. `heatTooHigh` → “Lower the heat; I want the color to move evenly, not spot-burn.”

**audio:** low value compared with color.

**equipment:** light-colored pan is easier for visual learning; dark cast iron makes rubric confidence lower.

---

## Béchamel

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Turn pale roux + milk into a smooth, neutral white sauce.
- **steps:** make pale roux; incorporate milk gradually/with a proven one- or two-pot method while whisking; bring to cooking temperature so starch fully thickens; season/strain as needed.
- **watchFors:** lumps; browned/scorched milk; sauce too thick/thin for use.
- **why:** it is the cleanest way to learn roux-thickened dairy sauce.

**starting ratio [INST, recipe-dependent]**
- Rouxbe example systems commonly land around a few tablespoons each butter/flour per ~2 cups liquid; do **not** make one ratio universal because desired béchamel ranges from thin coating to croquette binder.
- Teach ratio as a recipe target, and grade *final consistency*, not raw arithmetic.

**target visible**
- smooth, no flour lumps;
- pale/ivory;
- pours in a continuous ribbon and lightly coats spoon for medium sauce;
- no skin incorporated as lumps.

**ranked mistakes**
1. `lumpy` → “Stop adding milk and whisk the base smooth before you add more.”
2. `scorchedMilk` → “Don’t scrape the burnt bottom into the sauce; transfer the clean sauce and lower the heat.”
3. `tooThick` → “Whisk in more hot milk a little at a time.”
4. `tooThin` → “Give the starch time at a simmer before deciding it needs more thickener.”

**not visual:** raw flour taste, nutmeg/salt balance. Ask.

**reheat/hold:** gentle heat; loosen with milk; surface cover/contact film can prevent skin.

---

## Velouté

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Make a silky stock-based sauce with blond roux.
- **steps:** make blond roux; gradually incorporate flavorful white stock; simmer to smooth body; season if serving as final sauce.
- **why:** same thickening mechanics as béchamel, but the liquid’s flavor is exposed.

**reference recipe [SRC]:** Rouxbe publishes a basic example of 3 tbsp butter + 3 tbsp flour + about 2 cups white chicken stock, added progressively to a blond roux. Treat as a working lesson ratio, not immutable law. [SRC]

**target**
- smooth, velvety;
- pale cream/golden depending stock;
- coats lightly without paste-like heaviness.

**mistakes**
1. lumps;
2. weak/watery body → “Keep it at a gentle simmer and judge again after the starch fully cooks.”
3. paste-thick → “Loosen with hot stock.”
4. dark/burnt roux if a pale velouté intended.

**variations:** chicken, veal, fish, vegetable white stock; derivative flavoring is expected.

---

## Espagnole + Demi-Glace

**Course stance**
- Teach Espagnole once because it explains the classical brown-sauce family.
- Immediately show how **demi-glace** is built/used; that is more valuable than making students memorize a museum-piece taxonomy.
- Current culinary curricula still teach both classical leading sauces and demi-glace/contemporary sauces. [SRC]

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build a brown sauce from brown stock, browned aromatics/roux, tomato component, then concentration/straining; reduce with stock for demi-glace.
- **steps:** brown components without burning; build brown roux/base; add tomato and brown stock; simmer/skimming; strain; reduce further for demi as curriculum recipe specifies.
- **watchFors:** black burnt fond/roux; muddy greasy sauce; red tomato dominance; reducing before impurities/fat are managed.
- **why:** it teaches brown flavor development, stock quality, skimming, and long reduction.

**rubric**
- **better:** outcome.
- **target:** deep brown, glossy/clean rather than greasy, concentrated but pourable.
- **mistakes:** `burntBase` → discard/start affected stage; `greasy` → skim/defat; `underReduced` → continue gentle reduction; `overReduced` → loosen with compatible unsalted stock/water.
- **not visual:** bitterness, salt, stock quality; must taste.
- **equipment:** dark pan makes browning harder for AI; confidence lower.

---

## Sauce Tomate

**Course stance [DISAGREE]**
- Teach **classical French sauce tomate** as the mother-sauce application, but explicitly contrast it with Italian and modern tomato sauces. A student making a clean Italian tomato sauce is not “wrong”; it is a different sauce.
- Classical versions may use roux/stock/aromatics; contemporary tomato sauces often do not. [SRC]

**Lesson**
- sweat/brown aromatics as recipe intends; add tomato + liquid/base; simmer/reduce; pass/finish according to style.
- **target:** intentional tomato concentration, no scorched bottom, consistency suited to use.
- **mistakes:** scorching → “Lower the heat and stop scraping the burnt layer into the sauce.” `rawHarshTomato` contextual → “Give it more simmering before the final balance.” `overReducedPaste` → loosen.
- **camera strength:** strong for scorch, reduction, color; weak for acidity/sweetness/balance.

---

## Hollandaise

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build a warm egg-yolk/butter emulsion while controlling both heat and fat-addition rate.
- **steps:** make yolk/acid sabayon base; warm/whisk until appropriately aerated/thickened; add warm clarified butter gradually; adjust consistency/acid; hold only briefly and safely.
- **watchFors:** grainy curdled yolk; greasy separated butter; sauce too thick; excessive heat.
- **why:** it is the classic warm-emulsion stress test.

**target visible [SRC]**
- smooth, glossy, lemon-yellow;
- thick enough to coat but still pour;
- no oily pools; no grainy scrambled-egg particles.

**ranked mistakes**
1. `curdledYolk` — **Obs:** grainy cooked egg. **Say:** “That yolk has curdled; start the egg base again—don’t add more butter to this.” [SRC]
2. `splitEmulsion` — greasy separation. “Stop the butter and whisk in a few drops of water; rebuild the emulsion before you continue.” [SRC]
3. `butterTooFast` — “Slow the butter to a thin stream until the sauce tightens again.”
4. `tooThick` — “Whisk in a little water or lemon until it flows.”
5. `tooHot` — “Get the bowl off the heat and keep whisking.”

**temperature/safety [SRC]**
- Rouxbe notes yolks begin coagulating around 149°F/65°C and hollandaise becomes increasingly prone to curdling/splitting as it approaches/exceeds ~150°F.
- Rouxbe’s culinary hold guidance is roughly 120–145°F and no more than about 1.5 hours, while also acknowledging the food-safety tension of warm egg sauce. For Glutt home users, prefer **pasteurized eggs when serving higher-risk people and make hollandaise close to service rather than teaching long warm holding.**
- Do not let a visual rubric declare microbiological safety.

**camera strength:** extremely strong for split/curdled/gloss/body; weak for safe internal yolk treatment and taste.

---

## Mayonnaise (modern foundation)

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Build a stable cold oil-in-water emulsion using egg yolk/mustard/other emulsifier and acid.
- **steps:** combine aqueous/emulsifying base; add oil slowly while whisking/blending; once stable, oil can enter faster; adjust acid/water/seasoning.
- **watchFors:** oil dumped before emulsion forms; greasy break; overly stiff emulsion.
- **why:** it makes emulsion mechanics obvious with no heat variable.

**rubric**
- target: glossy, homogeneous, holds shape but spreads; no oil puddle.
- split rescue: start with a little water/acid or fresh yolk in clean bowl and slowly whisk broken mayo into it. [SRC]
- **safety:** pasteurized egg is safer for uncooked preparation, especially high-risk diners.

---

## Beurre Blanc (modern foundation)

**Lesson**
- **summary:** Emulsify cold butter into a reduced acidic aqueous base without overheating and splitting.
- **steps:** reduce wine/vinegar/shallot liquid; lower heat; whisk/swirl in cold butter piece by piece; maintain emulsion; season/strain if desired.
- **watchFors:** reduction dry before butter; boiling after butter begins; oil puddles.
- **why:** teaches butter emulsion and temperature control without egg yolk.

**rubric**
- target: glossy, opaque/creamy sauce with no floating butter oil.
- `split` → “Take it off the heat and whisk in a teaspoon of cool water, then add butter more slowly.”
- `tooHot` → “Off the heat now—the butter is melting out instead of emulsifying.”
- `baseTooDry` → “Add a spoon of water before the next butter cube; the emulsion needs an aqueous base.”

**Eggs**

**Sauces**
- Fond/deglaze/reduce + emulsion basics should come before complex sauce challenges.
- Roux → béchamel → velouté.
- Vinaigrette → mayonnaise before hollandaise.
- Reduction → beurre blanc.
- Hollandaise after emulsion + heat control.
- Espagnole/demi after stock/fond/reduction; do not put it early just because classical books do.

**Flavor**
- Taste safely → salt → acid → fat → umami → diagnose bland → rescue salty → finish with acid → balance sauce.
- `Taste as you go` must be conceptually before every flavor skill.

## B. Mastery: how many correct repetitions?

There is no defensible universal culinary number that turns one successful performance into a habit. **One pass should mean “demonstrated once,” not “learned.”**

My instructor standard for Glutt [INST]:

- **Simple discrete motor skill** (grip, claw, thermometer placement, crack egg): **3 clean repetitions across at least 2 sessions**.
- **Outcome skill with ingredient variability** (dice, julienne, scramble, fried egg): **3 successful outcomes across at least 2 sessions**, with at least one different ingredient/pan where relevant.
- **Heat judgement skill** (sauté, sear, reduce, brown butter): **4–5 successes across 3 sessions**, because a single pan/stove can be memorized.
- **Integrated challenge** (steak, pan sauce, omelette, cook without recipe): pass once = “completed”; repeat on a later day with one changed variable = “mastered.”
- Any **safety-critical mistake** (fingers under knife, raw/cooked cross-contamination, oil/fire hazard) means that attempt cannot count toward mastery.

Use states:
`introduced → demonstrated → repeatable → mastered`
instead of a binary learned/not learned.

## C. One-correction rule

**Keep it.** It matches good instruction *provided safety overrides everything*.

Priority:
1. Immediate safety.
2. Error that will irreversibly ruin the dish in the next few seconds.
3. Error with largest outcome cost.
4. Technique inefficiency/comfort.
5. Cosmetic precision.

Cases where two things are inseparable:
- **Claw grip:** “curl fingertips + pull thumb back” can be one compound safety correction if both are simultaneously in blade path.
- **Hollandaise:** `too hot + keep adding butter` must be stopped together: “Off the heat and stop the butter.”
- **Pan crowding + low heat:** remove food first; do not simultaneously tell beginner to blast heat under a wet crowded pan.
- **Burning pan:** take pan off heat before explaining anything else.

Otherwise, one correction at a time is better.

## D. Beginner mistakes books underweight

[INST]
1. **They obey the dial instead of the pan.**
2. **They continue cooking while deciding what to do.** First rescue action is often simply *remove heat*.
3. **They mistake more motion for more control**—constant stirring prevents browning; frantic knife speed prevents consistency.
4. **They season by memory, not by tasting.**
5. **They chase restaurant geometry before building a stable hand.**
6. **They protect four fingers and leave the thumb exposed.**
7. **They put too much food in one pan because batching feels inefficient.**
8. **They wait for a dramatic visual cue.** Many transitions (butter, egg set, reduction) should be acted on *before* the failure looks dramatic.
9. **They think “high heat = good sear” independent of thickness, pan mass, surface moisture, and internal temperature.**
10. **They assume a timer/recipe number is a measurement.** It is a forecast; the food supplies the measurement.

## E. Skills that cannot honestly be “watched learned” by this system

### Coachable but not visually certifiable
- Read a recipe
- Season as you go
- Taste as you go
- Balance a sauce
- Salt properly (beyond distribution/process)
- Understand acid/fat/umami
- Know why food tastes bland
- Fix bland food
- Fix oversalted food
- Finish with acid
- Make substitutions
- Scale a recipe
- Cook without a recipe (integrated reasoning can be assessed, but not vision alone)

### Partially watchable
- Doneness: visible cues help, but protein safety requires temperature.
- Reduce: viscosity/volume visible; concentration/taste not.
- Pan sauce: structure visible; balance not.
- Meat seasoning: distribution visible; salt level not.
- Soft/hard boiled eggs: result only after opening.
- Resting: action/timing visible; internal thermal behavior needs thermometer/context.

**Do not force these into fake pose-classifier rubrics.** Their “assessment” should be conversation + observed action + outcome.

## F. Where instructors genuinely disagree

Chef should explicitly carry these as accepted branches:
- pinch grip vs task-specific pointer/handle grips;
- rock vs push/pull cutting;
- traditional onion horizontal cuts vs safer no-horizontal method;
- exact professional dice/julienne geometry vs home-cook functional consistency;
- pre-salting scrambled eggs;
- soft vs firm scrambled-egg standards;
- tender vs crispy fried-egg styles;
- poaching vortex/acid/straining;
- French vs American omelette;
- hot-start vs cold-start searing for some foods;
- how long small steaks need to rest and the explanation for why resting reduces juice loss;
- vinaigrette acid:oil ratio;
- whether classical mother-sauce taxonomy should organize modern sauce education.

When there is a branch, **ask intent first, then judge against that branch.**

# 5. WHERE AUDIO IS BETTER THAN SIGHT

Audio should be a **supporting sensor**, not an independent pass/fail signal until Glutt has its own labeled kitchen dataset.

High-value candidates:

1. **Pan ready / sear:** sharp immediate sizzle vs muted wet response. America's Test Kitchen has publicly demonstrated this as a practical auditory cue; research also confirms cooking sounds are recognizable categories. [SRC]
2. **Crowding:** strong sizzle collapsing into softer wet simmer can flag pan temperature loss/moisture accumulation.
3. **Simmer vs boil:** bubble density/rate has a strong sound signature.
4. **Butter:** active sizzle while water is cooking out, then quieter as free water diminishes. Use with color.
5. **Deep/shallow frying:** bubble/sizzle behavior contains information about water-oil interaction and oil state; physics research directly links bubble regimes and sound. [SRC]
6. **Knife through crisp/dense produce:** potentially useful for gross sharpness/force events, but do **not** tell a user “your knife is dull” from sound alone without Glutt-specific validation.
7. **Crispness:** acoustic features correlate strongly with perceived crispness in food-science research, but most studies measure fracture/eating sounds under controlled conditions. Interesting future capability, not ready as a cooking rubric by itself. [SRC]

**Audio failure rule:** background hood fans, music, pan material, microphone AGC, ingredient water, and distance all shift amplitude. Use audio to *raise/lower confidence* and ask a question; never fail a skill from decibels alone.

---

# 6. SAFETY OVERRIDE POLICY

Chef stops the lesson only when the dangerous state is **visibly established**, not inferred.

Immediate stop:
- finger visibly on/under knife edge;
- knife moving toward a bracing palm at close range;
- trying to catch falling knife;
- oil/fire flare with unsafe response;
- water about to be poured onto burning/hot oil;
- raw meat juices visibly contaminate ready-to-eat food with no cleanup;
- pan/handle visibly in an unstable spill position while hot fat is being manipulated;
- alcohol being poured from a bottle directly over an active flame/hot pan in a way that can flash back.

Warn/question, do not stop automatically:
- “pan looks hot”;
- “knife may be dull”;
- “meat looks underdone” (request thermometer);
- “oil may be near smoke point” unless visible smoke/fire;
- wrist angle looks awkward;
- cook is using a non-classical but controlled grip.

---

# 7. CONFIDENCE / VISIBILITY POLICY

I would not use one global threshold like 0.55 for every observation.

Each mistake should have:
- `requiredEvidence`
- `minimumFrames`
- `occlusionRule`
- `severity`
- `confidenceFloor`

**Instructor policy:**
- Ordinary technique correction: high evidence; if uncertain, ask for framing, do not correct.
- Contextual technique correction: require both the observation *and* lesson intent.
- Safety correction: lower tolerance for ambiguity **only when the geometry itself is visible**; if not visible, say “I can’t see your fingertips—show me before the next cut,” not “your fingers are under the blade.”

- instructor office-hours on julienne and dice accuracy
- Rouxbe gives classical dimensions while explicitly distinguishing formal precision from the looser functional consistency needed in home cooking.

**S3 — Serious Eats, onion and garlic technique**
- guides on slicing/dicing onions, including traditional and safer no-horizontal-cut methods
- current garlic-cut testing demonstrating that degree of cell damage changes pungency.

**S4 — ICE, pan sauce / saucier instruction**
- “Sauce-Making 102: How to Make a Pan Sauce”
- fond → deglaze → reduction; burnt fond carries bitterness.

**S5 — Rouxbe / Serious Eats / ThermoWorks, pan heat and searing**
- Rouxbe instructor guidance on pan preheating
- Serious Eats on sufficient sauté oil, cast-iron preheating, and cold-start techniques
- ThermoWorks on searing, carryover, thermometer use, and temperature-driven steak cooking.

**S6 — FoodSafety.gov**
- current Safe Minimum Internal Temperature chart: whole beef/pork/lamb/veal 145°F with 3-minute rest; ground meat 160°F; poultry 165°F; egg dishes 160°F; seafood 145°F consumer guidance.
- color is not a reliable safety indicator.

**S7 — Egg instruction**
- ICE: Jacques Pépin egg instruction, including flat-surface cracking and fresh eggs for poaching.
- Rouxbe: current Egg Basics / professional egg curriculum.
- American Egg Board: doneness and egg-safety guidance.
- Serious Eats: controlled tests on pre-salting scrambled eggs and multiple legitimate egg styles.

**S8 — Sauce / emulsion instruction**
- ICE: “Sauce-Making 101” and saucier series (emulsification, fond, reduction, temperature control), classical mother sauces and contemporary sauce classes.
- Rouxbe: current courses for roux, béchamel, velouté, hollandaise, beurre blanc, demi-glace, pan sauces.
- Auguste Escoffier School: current five mother sauces and roux instruction.

**S9 — Audio / food acoustics**
- Prada et al., 2024, *Behavior Research Methods*: validated kitchen/food sound database; participants broadly recognize kitchen/cooking sound categories.
- AIP / *Physics of Fluids*: bubble dynamics and sound in heated oil.
- America's Test Kitchen / NPR interview: sharp vs muted steak sizzle used as practical pan-heat cue.
- Food texture research: acoustic features correlate with crispness, though controlled fracture/eating conditions limit direct transfer to Glutt.

# 9. THE MOST IMPORTANT PRODUCT-INSTRUCTION DECISIONS, IN ONE PAGE

If only ten things from this deliverable make it into Chef, make them these:

1. **Ask the intended style before correcting a legitimate branch.**
2. **Never require perfect classical knife geometry for home mastery.**
3. **Accept both onion-dicing schools; never force the horizontal cut.**
4. **Stove dial numbers are not heat knowledge; pan response is.**
5. **Sear = browning, not sealing juices.**
6. **Protein color is not a safety test; thermometer outranks vision.**
7. **Taste skills cannot be certified by camera. Make them dialogue skills instead of fake visual rubrics.**
8. **One successful attempt is “demonstrated,” not “learned.”**
9. **Safety overrides the one-correction rule; otherwise one physical correction at a time is excellent teaching.**
10. **Mother sauces still deserve a place, but teach the mechanisms first: roux, reduction, emulsion, fond/deglaze—then classical applications, mayonnaise, beurre blanc, demi-glace, and pan sauce.**

# APPENDIX: what Glutt actually built from this, and what it did not

Added after the deliverable landed, so the gap between the recommendations and
the shipped catalog is written down rather than remembered.

## Applied

- **Mechanisms before the classical five.** Mother Sauces opens with roux and
  reaches back to reduction, fond and emulsion as prerequisites from earlier
  categories rather than teaching them twice. Hollandaise sits after mayonnaise.
- **Mayonnaise and beurre blanc added** as modern foundations, explicitly not as
  replacement mother sauces. Espagnole is taught with demi-glace.
- **Ask the style before judging.** Scrambled eggs, fried eggs and omelettes
  each carry an intent branch, so Chef asks which version the cook wants before
  she looks. Browning is a fault in a French omelette and the point of an
  American one, and she no longer has a house preference.
- **Outcome over process** for the knife cuts. Dice, mince, julienne, rough chop,
  garlic and herbs are all judged on the finished board.
- **Home tolerances stated separately** from professional ones, everywhere a
  number appears.
- **Severity ranking with its own confidence floors.** Safety corrections are
  sayable on thinner evidence than cosmetic ones.
- **Searing does not seal in juices**, and **colour is never a safety test**.
  Both are enforced in the meat rubrics rather than just avoided.
- **Pre-salting scrambled eggs is not a mistake** and appears nowhere as a fault.
- **The raw potato does not draw out salt.** The rescue lesson teaches dilution.
- **Roughly a fifth of the catalog has no visual rubric on purpose.** All of
  Flavor, all of Intuition, plus Balance a Sauce, Read a Recipe, Season as You
  Go, Judge Doneness and Understand Stove Heat. They are written lessons and
  nothing pretends to certify them.

## Not applied, and why

- **Merging "Know Why Food Tastes Bland" into "Fix Bland Food".** The overlap is
  real. Removing a node from a published map is a product decision, so the
  duplication is acknowledged in the lesson text instead and the merge is left
  open.
- **The title changes** (`Add Oil Correctly` to `Read Oil in a Pan`, `Cook With
  Butter` to `Read Butter in a Pan`, `Understand Stove Heat` to `Control Heat on
  Your Stove`). The rubrics already teach the reframed version. The titles are
  user facing and were not changed without asking.
- **The mastery ladder** (`introduced` to `demonstrated` to `repeatable` to
  `mastered`, with more repetitions for heat judgement than for discrete motor
  skills). This is right and it is a SwiftData migration plus a rebuild of the
  Progress tab, so it is its own piece of work rather than a content change.
- **Audio as an assessment signal.** The rubrics carry `audioSignals` and the
  prompt passes them through as context, but nothing listens yet. The
  microphone is open and unused, exactly as the brief described.

## The one thing still missing

Every skill above marked "no visual rubric" needs a **dialogue coaching mode**:
Chef teaches, watches the actions she can see, and asks about the rest, without
ever claiming to have judged the flavour. That mode does not exist. Until it
does, those skills are as good as a well written article, which is not what this
app is for.

# APPENDIX 2: learning without glasses

Added when the Skills tab stopped assuming a Meta headset.

## The insight that shaped it

Photo mode is not a degraded camera, it is a different lesson. Without glasses
Chef cannot narrate over somebody's hands while they work, so the shape becomes:
she teaches, they check themselves, they show her. Every instruction changes
with it. "Look down at your hand and turn it slowly" is a sentence that only
makes sense when the camera is on your face and is nonsense when the camera is
the thing you are holding.

For several skills photos are simply **better**:

- **The knife grip.** Nobody can see both faces of a blade from their own eyes,
  which is the geometric problem the whole rubric was built around. A phone
  takes one photo of each side and the problem disappears.
- **Every outcome-judged cut.** A board of finished dice photographs better
  standing over it, in good light, than it ever looks through a moving
  first-person view.

## What is shared, and what is not

Shared, unchanged: the rubric, the ranked mistakes, the severity ladder, the
confidence floors, the visibility gating, and every authored correction.
`SkillVisualAssessor.assess(check:frames:)` takes `[Data]` and never cared where
the bytes came from, so a photographed attempt is judged against exactly the
same standard and hears exactly the same sentence.

Different per mode: `framingInstruction` vs `photoFraming`, the setup line's
opener, the callout title and body, the primary action, and the number of views
asked for. Two deliberate photographs, not three, because three photographs with
a knife in one hand is a chore where three live frames were free.

## Detecting it

There is no passive signal for "has glasses". The obvious candidate,
`GlassesSupport.isAvailable`, means the toolkit configured cleanly, which is
true on every install whether or not the cook owns anything; using it put the
entire world into a mode that needs hardware. Connection state is otherwise only
knowable after starting a session, far too late to decide what a lesson screen
should offer.

So: **photos by default**, and the first time a pair of glasses actually
connects anywhere in the app that fact is remembered and Skills opens in
watching mode from then on. A cook who picks for themselves is never overridden,
including when they pick the mode they are already in.

## Still to do

- Photo framing is authored for the knife skills. Everything else falls back to
  the live instruction, which is right where the two coincide (looking down at a
  board) and wrong where they do not.
- Chef does not yet talk during a photo lesson. The teaching happens on the
  lesson screen and the check happens in the sheet, with no voice between them.
